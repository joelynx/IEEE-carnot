\begin{abstract}
Team carnot's system for Task~1 of the FinReason Cup 2026 answers template-generated financial questions with a final answer and named intermediate values; unreleased templates carry half of the test score. A language model writes a Python program per question; it is executed with exact rational arithmetic, and its outputs are rendered from the published specification. Questions without a train counterpart get a five-sample per-field vote. On the development leaderboard the final system, R4, scores 1.000000 on final answers and 0.999167 on reasoning steps (strongest organiser baseline: 0.984444 and 0.990000). The residual errors we inspected were misread slot conventions. On familiar templates, a run without worked examples lost two final answers in 580, re-executing one run's programs in double precision lost three or four (depending on how floats are written), and the five-sample vote returned its first sample's answers. Changes aimed at the hidden templates were pre-registered and screened on fixed splits with a lockbox, but only for harm on familiar templates: hidden-template gains could not be measured, and our synthetic hidden-like sets were too easy. In the official results R4 ranks 17th of 27 teams, with combined final-answer correctness 0.985304 and hidden-stratum correctness 0.976025. If the 348 questions routed as novel are the hidden ones, the published scores imply eight wrong finals among them and three among the 580 others; the 348 are longer than the released questions, use conditional wording more often, and the five samples disagreed on them more often. Code, prompts, logs and response cache are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Task~1 of the FinReason Cup 2026~\cite{finreason2026overview,finreason2026task1overview} asks a system to answer template-generated financial calculation questions and to expose its intermediate values. Each question comes with a specification of the final answer (a unit and a rounding rule, or an enumeration of allowed strings) and an ordered list of named intermediate \emph{slots}, each with a one-sentence description and its own specification. A prediction is scored by exact match on two measures: final-answer correctness (FAC) and a checkpoint score, the share of a question's slots whose value matches the reference. The test set has two strata. The \emph{seen} stratum comes from the 290 templates for which labelled data were released; the \emph{hidden} stratum comes from templates that were never released. The two strata are weighted equally in the ranking, and hidden FAC is the first tie-breaker. The task builds on FinChain~\cite{xie-etal-2026-finchain}, a symbolic benchmark of parameterised financial templates with executable solutions over the same 12 domains and 58 topics. The organisers describe FinChain as a provenance and novelty reference only; we did not use it in the system (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}).

Scoring is exact, and that shaped the design: a half-up field is compared after quantisation to the published places and an exact field as a rational number, so a value that is off in its last digit scores no better than a wrong method, and one malformed value invalidates the whole file. We therefore execute programs with exact rational arithmetic and render each field from its published specification. Transcendental functions and root-finding remain finite-precision approximations, which are allowed only in half-up fields (Section~\ref{sec:sandbox}). Neither exact execution nor rendering checks that a program computes the right quantity, and the residual errors we inspected are of that kind: they concern the organisers' conventions, for example whether an ``annual compound multiplier'' covers one year or the whole horizon. Such conventions can be learned for seen templates and are unknown for hidden ones.

Program-of-thought prompting and self-consistency are established methods, and we propose no new prompting technique. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a pipeline in which every submitted string is the rendered value of an executed program, and whose final system, R4, scores 1.000000 (final answer) and 0.999167 (reasoning steps) on the development leaderboard, above the organisers' published baselines (Sections~\ref{sec:system} and~\ref{sec:results});
\item an error and component analysis on labelled data: the residual errors are misread conventions, a stronger model fixed one convention but broke other slots, and borrowing conventions from other templates failed because slot names are not used consistently (Sections~\ref{sec:components}, \ref{sec:negative} and~\ref{sec:errors});
\item an evaluation design with fixed splits, a lockbox, paired bootstrap comparisons and pre-registration, which tests for detectable harm on familiar templates and cannot measure hidden-template gains (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:post});
\item replayable submissions and a public record: the R3 and R4 test submissions regenerate byte-identically from the released response cache, and the experiment log is public (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}).
\end{enumerate}
The official test results place R4 17th of 27 teams with a hidden-stratum FAC of 0.976025 (Section~\ref{sec:test}). Our development checks did not anticipate this gap (Section~\ref{sec:hidden}).

\paragraph*{Related work}
Program-aided and program-of-thought prompting~\cite{gao2023palprogramaidedlanguagemodels,chen2023programthoughtspromptingdisentangling} separate reasoning from computation by having the model write code that an interpreter runs; we add exact rational arithmetic and rendering from the published specification. Chain-of-thought prompting~\cite{NEURIPS2022_9d560961} and self-consistency decoding~\cite{wang2023selfconsistencyimproveschainthought} motivate the sampled programs and the plurality vote on the novel route. Earlier financial numerical-reasoning benchmarks such as FinQA~\cite{chen-etal-2021-finqa}, TAT-QA~\cite{zhu-etal-2021-tat} and ConvFinQA~\cite{chen-etal-2022-convfinqa} score final answers over tables and text; FinChain~\cite{xie-etal-2026-finchain} and this task also score the intermediate chain.

\section{Task, Data and Scoring}\label{sec:task}

\subsection{Records and rendering conventions}
A question record holds the question text, an \id{answer\_spec} and a \id{trace\_spec}. The text ends with a generated ``Answer format'' sentence. A decimal specification names a unit from a fixed set and a rounding mode, either \emph{exact} or \emph{half-up} to a given number of places; an enum specification lists the allowed strings. The trace specification is an ordered list of slots, each with a \id{slot\_id}, a free-text description and its own specification. Gold values are strings: half-up values are padded to the published places; exact values are a minimal decimal when the denominator divides a power of ten and otherwise an irreducible fraction such as \id{3/11}; enums are case-sensitive. Percent-type units are in percentage points (a premium of 6 percent is \id{6}), whereas ratios are plain fractions.

\subsection{Data}
We use the FinReason Cup 2026 Task~1 release, \url{https://the-finai.github.io/IEEE-bigdata-cup/task1/}, licensed under CC BY 4.0 (\url{https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/}); we made no changes to the data, and every run hash-checks the files against the release manifests. The development data contract is \id{task1-v4} and the test release is \id{task1-v4-test-questions-20260901-r2}. The release has 2,900 train questions (ten per template), 290 development questions (one per template), 580 development-leaderboard questions without gold and 928 test questions without gold. The 290 seen templates span 58 topics in 12 domains, with 116 basic, 116 intermediate and 58 advanced templates. Train final answers are 2,700 decimals and 200 enums; train questions carry 5,180 slot instances (320 questions have none). Train and development together hold 8,888 gold fields: 3,091 half-up, 5,379 exact and 418 enum. The test file has 1,492 slots over 928 questions, 64 of which have no slot.

\subsection{Scoring and ranking}
Scores come from the organisers' frozen scorer, version \id{finreason.task1.scorer/2.0.0} at toolkit commit \id{983537c}, which they confirmed is the test scorer. It matches each field exactly: enum strings must be equal; exact decimals are compared as rational numbers; half-up decimals are quantised to the published places with round-half-up on both sides and then compared. A case scores FAC~$=1$ if its final answer matches. Its checkpoint score is the number of matching slots divided by the number of published slots; cases without slots are excluded from the checkpoint metric. Scores aggregate hierarchically: the mean over the cases of a template, the mean over templates within a (domain, topic, difficulty) cell, a difficulty-weighted mean per topic (seen weights basic 2/5, intermediate 2/5, advanced 1/5; hidden weights 1/2 and 1/2), the mean over topics per domain, and the mean over the 12 domains. A file with one invalid row (an unknown slot, a duplicate, an out-of-order step, a malformed decimal or an enum outside the vocabulary) is rejected as a whole. The organisers confirmed that the final ranking is the descending lexicographic order of (combined FAC, hidden FAC, combined checkpoint), each key quantised to six decimal places with round-half-even, where ``combined'' is the equal-weighted mean of the seen and hidden strata; ties share a rank.

\subsection{Seen and hidden strata}\label{sec:strata}
The scorer's frozen counts give 580 seen and 348 hidden test questions. A rule built from train alone (Section~\ref{sec:routing}) flags exactly 348 test questions as novel and none of the train, development, lockbox or leaderboard questions; each of the 290 released templates matches exactly two of the other 580. We assume, but cannot verify, that the 348 are the hidden questions; we call them the \emph{novel route} and the other 580 the \emph{seen route}. The public fields of the 348 differ markedly from train (Section~\ref{sec:hidden}). No labelled example of a hidden template is public, so the hidden stratum, half of the combined score and the first tie-breaker, cannot be measured with released data.

\subsection{Pipeline}
For every question the solver builds a prompt (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}) from four parts: a fixed system prompt with the rules of the sandbox, the unit conventions and the instruction to follow the question's own definitions and scenario-local rules; three worked examples (Section~\ref{sec:exemplars}); the question verbatim; and a table of required outputs. The table lists the key \id{final} and every slot id, each with its description and a short rendering of its specification (for example ``number in percent, percentage points (5.25 means 5.25\,\%); checked as an exact value (return it unrounded)''). The model answers with one Python code block defining \id{solve()}, which returns a dictionary with exactly those keys.

The sandbox executes the program and the formatter renders each value from its specification; when execution fails, a key is missing or a value cannot be rendered, up to two retries per sample re-prompt with the failing program and the error text. With $N>1$ samples a per-field plurality vote selects the rendered strings (Section~\ref{sec:voting}). Each row then passes the toolkit's \id{validate\_prediction\_record} before it is written. A field that is still invalid is omitted rather than edited (a final becomes \id{null}), which costs only that field. No run needed this fallback: every test submission has a final for all 928 questions and a value for all 1,492 slots. Submissions are produced with the toolkit's own \id{validate}, \id{package} and \id{validate-zip} commands.

\subsection{Verified worked examples}\label{sec:exemplars}
The three worked examples are fixed. We defined three categories from public specification fields only: (A) a decimal final with at least two exact slots including a percent or ratio unit; (B) an enum final with at least one decimal slot; (C) a decimal final with at least one half-up slot. For each category, templates of the exemplar pool (a subset of the tuning split, Section~\ref{sec:splits}) are visited in a seeded order and one case per template is solved zero-shot by the base solver; a program is kept only if the official scorer gives a correct final and every slot correct against train gold. Two verified programs per category, from distinct templates, are kept as a primary and a backup; no candidate was rejected. Every prompt shows the three primaries; in leave-template-out evaluation a backup replaces a primary that shares the question's template. There is no retrieval: as deployed, every question gets the same three examples, including the questions of those three templates.

\subsection{Exact-arithmetic sandbox}\label{sec:sandbox}
Programs run under a code policy and an arithmetic rewrite. An AST whitelist admits imports of \id{math}, \id{fractions}, \id{decimal}, \id{itertools}, \id{functools} and a helper module, and rejects classes, \id{with} blocks, \id{eval}, \id{exec}, \id{open}, dunder names and private attributes. The AST is then rewritten so that every float literal becomes an exact rational (\id{0.05} becomes \id{Q("0.05")}) and every arithmetic operator becomes an exact helper; division of integers is exact and \id{float()} raises. The helper module provides \id{sqrt}, \id{ln}, \id{exp}, \id{erf}, the standard normal CDF, bisection root-finding and half-up rounding. Only rational operations are exact: irrational results, and anything computed from them, are 60-digit decimals, and bisection stops at a tolerance of $10^{-45}$ unless the program sets another; the formatter accepts such values only in half-up fields. Programs execute in a WSL2 Ubuntu guest inside \id{unshare -rmn} (new user, mount and network namespaces; no network; Windows drives hidden behind a tmpfs), one child process per program with resource limits and a five-second timeout.

\subsection{Spec-driven formatter and validator gate}
The formatter receives each returned value and emits the submitted string. An exact field becomes a minimal finite decimal when the denominator is of the form $2^a5^b$ and otherwise an irreducible \id{n/d}; a half-up field is quantised with round-half-up to the published places and padded; an enum is checked for membership. In an exact field an inexact value (a float or a 60-digit decimal) is a rendering error that triggers a retry; a half-up field rounds it, and a float is flagged in the log. A round-trip test re-renders all 8,888 gold values of train and development, and every one comes back byte-identical. Since no other component writes a value and the validator checks every row, each submitted string is the rendered value of an executed program.

\subsection{Self-consistency}\label{sec:voting}
With $N$ samples the solver takes, for each field independently, the plurality of the rendered strings over the samples that rendered that field. Candidates count as equal when their rendered strings are identical, which for rendered values coincides with the scorer's criterion, so no tolerance is added. A tie goes to the string held by the sample that agrees with the per-field plurality on the most fields, then to the lowest sample index. A field with no valid candidate is omitted. Each field therefore comes from an executed program, but a row may combine fields of several programs; the validator checks each field's format and does not check relations between slots.

\subsection{Routing rule}\label{sec:routing}
Rule \id{novel\_vs\_train\_v1} declares a question novel if neither its number-masked text skeleton (lower-cased, whitespace-normalised, the ``Answer format'' sentence removed, every number replaced by \id{\#}) nor its ordered list of (slot id, description) pairs occurs among the 2,900 train questions. It sends 348 test questions, and no train, development, lockbox, tuning-eval or leaderboard question, to the novel route.

\subsection{Model access and reproducibility}\label{sec:model}
All model calls go to Claude models through the Claude Code command-line interface (version 2.1.296) in headless mode on a Claude subscription: one subprocess per call with our own system prompt, no tools, a single turn and JSON output, from an empty working directory with a scrubbed environment. The interface exposes no temperature or seed. The samples of a question are therefore separate calls with the same prompt, and reproducibility comes from a response cache keyed by model, effort, interface version, flag version, the hashes of the system and user prompts, the sample index and the attempt tag. Replaying the R3 and R4 test submissions from this cache reproduced the uploaded files byte for byte (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}). The returned model identifier was recorded for every call; across all 69,277 cached calls, including the post-submission experiments and the ablation of Section~\ref{sec:components}, it equals the requested identifier.

\subsection{Systems R0 to R4}\label{sec:systems}
Table~\ref{tab:systems} defines the systems R0 to R4; R1 introduces the routing rule. \emph{Unit hint v2} (R3 on the novel route, R4 on both routes) describes a \id{dimensionless} field in the required-outputs table as ``a pure number with no unit; keep the scale of the question's own quantities (do not rescale percentages to fractions unless the description says so)'' instead of ``a plain number (0.0525, not 5.25)''. \emph{Echo notes} (R4, both routes): when a sentence of the question repeats a slot description verbatim (after lower-casing, whitespace normalisation and dropping the final period), the required-outputs table states that the sentence belongs to that slot and is not the definition of the final answer. The echo trigger fires on none of the 348 novel-route test questions, so R4's novel-route answers are R3's. The system prompt was not changed and still says that outputs in ratio, multiple or dimensionless units are plain numbers; the per-field hint is more specific, and it was enough to change the scale that the programs returned (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Splits, lockbox and modes}\label{sec:splits}
Train and development, eleven labelled cases per template, were split once with a recorded seed. The \emph{lockbox} (all 290 development cases plus two train cases per template, 870 cases) is reserved for confirming candidates. Four logged looks were allowed and two were used; in the first, three cases were inspected (Section~\ref{sec:errors}), and later results were read as aggregates only. The \emph{tuning} split holds the other eight train cases per template (2,320). Of these, \emph{tuning-eval} is two fixed cases per template (580), the \emph{exemplar pool} is the rest (1,740) and the only source of worked examples, and \emph{tuning-noex} is tuning minus the six exemplar cases (2,314). Every template appears in every split. The development leaderboard was used twice: as a pipeline check for R0 and to decide the adoption of R4.

Labelled evaluations run in two modes. S-mode uses the fixed examples as deployed; H-mode excludes examples from the question's own template. Because the system has no retrieval, H-mode changes the worked examples of only 6 of 580 tuning-eval and 21 of 2,314 tuning-noex questions and otherwise differs in the resampling unit, so agreement between the modes is a sensitivity check and not an independent replication.

\subsection{Comparisons and decision rules}\label{sec:compare}
Labelled runs are scored with the toolkit's \id{score\_submission}. For a comparison, per-case weights are obtained from the scorer by one-hot probing, each run's score is recomputed from them and checked against the toolkit's report, and the difference (candidate minus baseline) is bootstrapped with shared resample indices (10,000 replicates, fixed seed; cases within templates in S-mode, templates within topics in H-mode). Run-to-run noise was measured with three R0 runs on tuning-eval with fresh samples (FAC 1.000000, 0.998333 and 1.000000), which gives $\sigma_{\mathrm{run}}(\mathrm{FAC})=0.000962$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{run}}(\mathrm{checkpoint})=0.000481$. Three runs of one configuration give only a rough noise scale for the thresholds below.

Two rules were fixed before any candidate was run. The \emph{improvement rule} adopts a candidate if the paired 95\,\% interval of $\Delta$ excludes 0, $\Delta>2\sigma_{\mathrm{run}}$, and one subsequent lockbox look gives $\Delta>0$. Changes aimed at the hidden stratum fall under the \emph{adoption rule} (recorded in the pre-registration as a non-inferiority rule). Such a change is pre-registered with its configuration and code hashes and its pass and fail actions before any of its outputs exist, and it acts only through the train-derived routing rule. Its novel-route configuration is then applied to every tuning-eval question in both modes (``forced''), and neither score may be detectably lower: $\Delta\ge-2\sigma_{\mathrm{run}}$ and an interval whose upper bound is $\ge0$, in all four comparisons. The decision uses no statistic computed on test outputs; Section~\ref{sec:integrity} discloses one statistic of a test run that a written rationale mentioned. The author approved each candidate's specification before it was built, and every pre-registration, result and incident is logged.

The adoption rule tests only for detectable degradation, with a point-estimate tolerance, and does not establish statistical non-inferiority: an interval that contains 0 can also contain substantial harm (R2's H-mode checkpoint interval reaches $-0.006667$). Adoption under it rests on prior evidence and the absence of detected harm on familiar templates. The supplementary material (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}) gives the full protocol.

\subsection{Pre-registered decisions}\label{sec:decisions}
R1 and R3 passed all four comparisons of the adoption rule, with no FAC change and small checkpoint gains ($+1/1200$ and $+1/900$; Table~\ref{tab:decisions}); R3's S-mode interval is degenerate because both changed cases lie in one template. R2 passed as well and was not submitted. Only the R3 S-mode and the R4 seen-route checkpoint gains meet the pre-declared improvement criteria (an interval that excludes 0 and $\Delta>2\sigma_{\mathrm{run}}$) on tuning data, and the lockbox step of that rule confirmed neither, so we call none of these differences significant.

\subsection{The hidden-stratum gap}\label{sec:hidden}
No reference answer of a hidden template is public, so the cause of the gap cannot be established.

\emph{Size.} Under the assumption of Section~\ref{sec:strata}, the published scores determine how the eleven wrong finals divide. A seen-stratum question carries one of four weights, set by the number of topics in its domain, and only one combination of such weights reproduces the seen-stratum loss within the rounding of the published scores (the supplementary material gives the derivation): R4 missed 3 of 580 seen-stratum finals and 8 of 348 hidden-stratum finals. Eight teams reached a hidden FAC of 1.000000.

\emph{Form.} In public fields the novel-route questions differ from train. Their 149 distinct slot ids never occur in train. All 456 of their slots are rounded half-up, whereas 4,850 of the 5,180 train slots are exact, and 84 of 348 have an enum final, against 200 of 2,900. The median novel-route question has 895 characters and, before its answer-format note, six sentences and seven numbers; the median train question has 556 characters, four sentences and four numbers. A conditional cue (``if'', ``otherwise'', ``at least'', ``exceed'' and similar) occurs in 168 of 348, against 470 of 2,900, and wording that asks for the input at which a defined quantity meets a target (``at which'', ``what \ldots\ makes'', ``that makes'') occurs in 27 of 348 and in no train question.

\emph{Sample agreement.} After the results were published we computed one descriptive statistic on our own test outputs (disclosure~6 in Section~\ref{sec:integrity}). The five samples of the novel route disagreed on the final answer in 10 of the 348 novel-route questions (eight 4--1 and two 3--2 votes) and on 23 of the 804 fields. With the same configuration forced onto tuning-eval they disagreed on no final and on 1 of 1,616 fields. The samples thus disagree more often on the novel route than on familiar templates, but we cannot tell whether the eight wrong finals were split or unanimous votes.

\emph{Hypotheses.} First, longer questions with more conditional rules leave more room to misread one rule, which is the kind of error we found on labelled data (Section~\ref{sec:errors}) and which voting does not remove when all samples share the misreading. Second, the worked examples, the unit hints and the echo rule were drawn from and checked on the released templates, whose questions are shorter and whose slots are mostly exact, so nothing in the prompt was fitted to the form of the novel-route questions. Our synthetic panels were too easy to expose such errors in advance (Sections~\ref{sec:negative} and~\ref{sec:post}). The specifications give no reason to suspect arithmetic or rendering, although no hidden case can be inspected: every novel-route question with a numeric final prescribes exact intermediate arithmetic and half-up rounding of the final value, which is what the sandbox and the formatter do.

\section{Component Analysis}\label{sec:components}
\input{tables/components}
Table~\ref{tab:components} examines four components on tuning-eval, which holds familiar templates only. The comparisons are not equally controlled: two rescore stored runs, one adds a run made five weeks later, and one re-executes stored programs. No run in the table needed a retry, and all blocks but the last use the same exact arithmetic and formatter.

\emph{Worked examples.} A pre-registered run of R0 without its three worked examples, made for this analysis, missed five slots, as R0 did and in the same three conventions, and two finals. Both finals show the tax confusion of Section~\ref{sec:echo}; with the examples, one of these two questions was missed in one of the three low-effort runs and in one of the five medium-effort samples. The paired difference to R0 is $-1/300$ in FAC, with a 95\,\% interval of $[-0.006667,0]$, and 0 in checkpoint; the two runs were made five weeks apart through the same interface version and model identifier, so a change of the served model in between cannot be excluded and is confounded with the ablation. Field by field, the run without examples differs from the first R0 run in 4 of 1,616 fields, these two finals and two slots; this single comparison suggests, and cannot establish, that the examples make the tax confusion rarer.

\emph{Effort.} Scored alone, each of the five medium-effort samples of R1 has four or five slot misses and at most one wrong final, which is the range of the three low-effort R0 runs.

\emph{Sampling.} The five-sample vote of R1 forced returns exactly the fields of the first sample. The samples disagree on 2 of 1,616 fields, a final in one sample and a slot in another; the vote corrects these two deviations and otherwise returns what all five samples returned. In the forced runs of R2, R3 and R4 the vote likewise returns the fields of its first sample.

\emph{Exact arithmetic.} A pre-registered replay executed the 580 programs of the first R0 run again without a model call: once exactly, which reproduced every stored field, and once in IEEE binary64 arithmetic under the same isolation. The formatter refuses a float in an exact field, so three ways of writing one were fixed in advance (last block of Table~\ref{tab:components}); the third writes a reconstructed fraction when the value is not a short decimal. Every rule loses three half-up finals, each an exact tie at the last published place that the computed double falls just short of: 22.815 is computed as 22.814999999999998 and rounds to 22.81. The first rule also loses an exact final, written as 100.00000000000001. All lost slots are exact fields. Of the 93 exact slots whose gold is a fraction, the first two rules lose every one and the third loses 29; of the 872 exact decimal slots that the exact run matches, the three rules lose 80, 29 and 30. R0's run on tuning-noex holds these 580 programs and 1,734 others; among the others every rule loses 10 half-up finals, all of them exact ties. These figures describe programs written for an exact pipeline and three renderings, not every floating-point pipeline. On the novel route every numeric field is rounded half-up, so the exposure there would be to such ties.

\section{Variant Experiments}\label{sec:negative}

\subsection{A stronger model on the novel route (R2)}\label{sec:opus}
Every residual R1 error on tuning-eval was a convention error. A pre-registered diagnostic took the four tuning-eval cases in which R1 still missed a slot (two templates), drew ten fresh samples per case and arm with the same prompt, examples and sandbox, and compared \id{claude-sonnet-5-5} with \id{claude-opus-5-5}, both at medium effort. On the sign convention of \id{factor\_ratio\_trace} fresh Sonnet samples were right in 0 of 20 and Opus in 20 of 20; on the variance units of \id{variance\_trace} both were right in 0 of 20; all 80 non-target fields were right in both arms; votes of five were right in 0 of 8 and 4 of 8. The diagnostic's pre-registered rule favoured Opus, although Opus had fixed only one of the two conventions. Forced onto tuning-eval, R2 differed from R1 on five slots: two fixed (the sign convention) and three broken, namely two \id{growth\_multiplier\_trace} slots, where the description says ``annual compound multiplier'' but the gold is the cumulative multiplier over the horizon and Opus returns the annual factor, and one \id{eligible\_rebate\_trace} slot lost on a 3--2 vote. One reading is that Opus follows slot descriptions more literally, which would match the gold for the sign convention and contradict it for the annual multiplier; two conventions are too few to establish this.

\subsection{A synthetic hidden-like labelled set}
We tried to build a hidden-like labelled set of new templates in the competition's format. A model outside the comparison, \id{claude-fable-5-1}, wrote them after seeing tuning-set questions of the assigned topic only. Their exact reference programs ran in our sandbox with parameters drawn by our own seeded code, every record passed the toolkit validators, and every question was novel under the routing rule. To filter trivial templates, a pre-registered probe ran the smallest model, \id{claude-haiku-5-5}, through the same pipeline on four instances per template; a template counted as trivial if every field of every instance was right. Under train-like rules the probe found 24 of 25 authored templates trivial, which left one non-trivial template against a pre-registered floor of 12. Under stricter rules (advanced only, at least five steps, a threshold decision, a solve-for or multi-period timing, three or four slots) all 5 templates of a pilot were trivial, and the work stopped. Nothing from this set was used in any submitted system.

This process did not produce a set that separates systems: the fully specified problems that the model wrote were easy for our pipeline. Leaving conventions unstated would not help, because the gold would then follow the author's conventions rather than the organisers'.

\subsection{Cross-template convention examples}
Novel-route questions use slot ids that never occur in train (Section~\ref{sec:hidden}), so we tried to teach convention reading by example. Candidate \id{conventions\_v1} combined unit hint v2 with \emph{slot-convention examples}: for each required slot, up to two questions from \emph{other} templates of the tuning pool with the same slot id or the same description, shown with the slot's reference value. It was evaluated as \id{e\_conv} against the baseline \id{e\_base} (Sonnet, medium effort, one sample: the first sample of R1's novel route) on tuning-noex in H-mode and lost (Table~\ref{tab:decisions}): 11 slots fixed, 8 broken and 2 finals broken. Of these changes, the unit hint alone fixed 8 slots and broke none on the 80 dimensionless-field questions whose prompt differed only by the hint, while the cross-template examples fixed 3 slots and broke 8, mostly \id{factor\_ratio\_trace} in a different template, plus 2 tax finals. The same slot name carries different conventions in different templates, so the borrowed examples misled more often than they helped. The candidate was rejected without further tuning, and R1 stayed.

\subsection{The unit hint (adopted as R3)}
The original \id{dimensionless} hint (Section~\ref{sec:systems}) was our own error. In train, the gold values of dimensionless fields are counts, growth multipliers, pip differences, sentiment components and squares of the question's own quantities (a variance of 225 from weighted deviations that sum to 15 percent), never percentages rescaled to fractions. The hint-only candidate was chosen after the \id{conventions\_v1} result, so its tuning evidence is biased; the decision rested, as pre-registered, on a breakage check on the 32 dimensionless-field questions not yet observed under the hint (1 slot fixed, 0 broken) and on one lockbox look. R3 carries the corrected hint on the novel route only. On test it re-solved only the novel-route questions with a dimensionless field (90 fresh calls); every other R3 answer comes from R1's cached responses.

\subsection{Echo notes (adopted in R4)}\label{sec:echo}
The only recurring wrong final on labelled data was the tax ``Report X'' confusion. Tax questions end with a sentence that repeats a slot description (``Report taxable income.'') while the final answer is the tax; models answered the sentence (on one tuning question with taxable income 400, the prediction was 400.00 and the gold tax 100.00). On train plus development the echo rule (Section~\ref{sec:systems}) fires on 22 questions of exactly two templates, \id{taxcalc.standard\_deduction} and \id{taxcalc.capital\_gains}, whose final is always the tax; it fires on 4 leaderboard, 4 seen-route test and 0 novel-route test questions. Together with the seen-route hint it fixed both tax finals on tuning-noex without breaking a final.

\section{Error Analysis}\label{sec:errors}
All case-level analysis uses tuning data, apart from the three lockbox cases disclosed below. R0 on tuning-eval in S-mode has 0 wrong finals and 5 slot misses of three kinds. (i)~\emph{Sign.} \id{factor\_ratio\_trace}, ``Exact signed factor-change ratio'', in a question that gives a signed rate change $c$ in percent and a signed sensitivity $s$ and asks for $V\cdot(c/100)\cdot s$: the gold is $c/100$, but the model also applied the sign of $s$ (predicted $-0.04$, gold $0.04$). (ii)~\emph{Units.} \id{variance\_trace}, ``Exact rational square used as the variance'', for a two-asset portfolio whose weighted deviations are in percent: the gold is 256 (percent squared), the prediction 0.0256. (iii)~\emph{Horizon.} \id{growth\_multiplier\_trace}, ``Exact annual compound multiplier'', in a three-year compounding question: the gold is $1.295029=1.09^3$, the prediction 1.09. The sign error appears twice, the units error twice and the horizon error once. Among the inspected errors we found no arithmetic-execution or formatting failure.

R3 forced has 2 slot misses, both the sign convention. R4's seen route on tuning-noex has 0 wrong finals and 6 slot misses: 5 sign errors in two single-factor portfolio templates and 1 horizon error; R0 on the same set has 14 slot misses and 2 wrong finals, both tax. The baseline of the convention experiments (\id{e\_base}, H-mode) has 14 slot misses and 3 wrong finals, all \id{taxcalc} (\id{standard\_deduction} twice, \id{capital\_gains} once): the taxable income or gain was returned instead of the tax. Lockbox look 1 (R0) had three wrong finals in 870 cases; those three were inspected individually and showed the same tax confusion. Every later fix was justified and measured on tuning data, where the tax confusion also occurs. Sampling did not fix the two systematic conventions we tested: 0 of 40 fresh samples at a fixed configuration got them right. R1 has one slot miss fewer than R0, but so does its first sample alone (Section~\ref{sec:components}), so the vote is not what removed it.

\section{Post-Submission Investigations}\label{sec:post}

After R4 was accepted we tested four variants of the novel route, with no further upload, test run or lockbox look. \emph{P} changes the system prompt to ask the program to bind each output to its quantity, sign and horizon. \emph{D} draws its five samples from five fixed roles (three Sonnet and two Opus) and keeps the per-field vote; \emph{J} selects a complete sample from the same pool. \emph{V} adds one Opus call that sees the candidate programs and must return a program whose outputs reproduce those of one candidate, so that it can select a candidate but cannot edit a value.

Table~\ref{tab:post} gives the results and the supplementary material the full protocol. On a pilot of 24 synthetic families of four instances, every difference lay in one amortizing-bond family whose wording admits a one-time or a recurring reading of a rate change. In a pre-registered continuation we replayed the pilot from the cache and checked the variants on tuning-eval, where each answered all 580 finals in both modes. A model then wrote 48 new families, each admitted only after a second implementation and a blind solver agreed with its reference. R4 made one final error in the first 24 families, a misapplied annual-to-quarterly growth conversion that the question stated. After the one design revision the protocol allowed, every system answered all 96 finals of the second 24, and the study stopped at this ceiling, as its pre-registered rule required. On a stress panel of paired variants every system was right when the bond schedule was stated explicitly; on the original wording and on a neutral paraphrase R4 scored 2 of 4 and V 4 of 4. The continuation's FAC criterion could not be met at ceiling and was replaced prospectively by a count-based rule, under which P lost no final answer and gained none on tuning-eval and on all 2,314 tuning-noex questions in both modes.

The variants' gains on these panels came mostly from the ambiguous bond wording, where ``correct'' means agreeing with an author's reading, and admitting a family only when a blind solver agrees with its reference biases the panels towards problems our models can solve. The panels therefore say little about what makes hidden templates hard.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\emph{Evaluation coverage.} Hidden-template performance could not be measured with released data, and the published result shows that it is where R4 falls short. Changes aimed at hidden questions were adopted on prior evidence and because no harm was detected on familiar templates; their effect on hidden templates was never measured. H-mode changes the examples of only a few questions, and on the synthetic panels, written and checked within one model family, the systems differed mostly on one ambiguous wording.

\emph{Causal attribution.} The component analysis covers familiar templates only and rests on few runs: its one run without examples is compared with baseline runs made five weeks earlier. The formatter was not ablated, the arithmetic replay covers three float renderings of programs written for an exact pipeline, and nothing identifies which component, if any, caused the hidden gap.

\emph{Scope of the fixes.} R3's gain is small and slot-level (the third ranking key), with a lockbox interval that includes 0; R4's echo rule fixes one template family and never fires on the novel route; and we found no general fix for the remaining convention errors (sign conventions, annual against cumulative multipliers, taxable amount against tax).

The system reaches near-perfect development scores with exact rational execution and specification-based rendering, and its R3 and R4 test submissions regenerate from the released cache. On the official test it falls short on hidden templates, and none of our checks revealed that gap in advance. Further work on this pipeline should start with held-out templates fixed before development, and should test whether disagreement among the samples marks the wrongly answered questions, which we could not tell on the test set.
